Reaction enthalpy

Define a reaction enthalpy and distinguish the two ways of determining it.

Defining reaction enthalpy

ΔH describes the enthalpy change of the entire system; ΔrH describes the change per unit extent of the reaction as written. At constant temperature and pressure, for a single reaction, dH = ΔrH dξ. If ΔrH is constant, ΔH = (ξf − ξi) ΔrH. Changing the amount transformed changes ΔH; multiplying the chemical equation changes the value of ΔrH associated with that equation.

Exothermic does not necessarily mean spontaneous

The sign of the reaction enthalpy describes the heat balance, but is insufficient to determine the direction of change. This question is explored in theoptional extension, beyond the syllabus.

Standard reaction enthalpy

The standard reaction enthalpy ΔrH° refers to a reaction in which reactants in their standard states become products in their standard states.

ΔH = Qp ≈ ξ ΔrH°

This expression applies to the reaction contribution at constant temperature and pressure, with pressure–volume work only, zero initial extent, and ΔrH approximated by a constant standard value. ξ is the extent of the process. ΔH is in J; ΔrH° is in J·mol⁻¹. If ΔrH° < 0, the reaction is exothermic; if ΔrH° > 0, it is endothermic.

Ellingham approximation: ΔrH° is treated as constant over the temperature interval studied.

For a process with zero initial extent, Qreaction ≈ ξΔrH°: the total heat depends on the amount transformed. To recover the molar quantity, divide by ξ, determined from the reaction-extent table. For a complete reaction, use the limiting reactant.

Specific reaction enthalpies

Phase-transition enthalpy

Enthalpy change associated with a change in physical state: melting, vaporisation or sublimation.

Choose the phase transition

→

At fixed pressure, the temperature of a pure substance stays constant during an equilibrium phase transition. Reversing the phase transition at the same temperature reverses the sign of its enthalpy.

H₂O(s) → H₂O(l) : ΔfusH°
H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) : ΔvapH°

Elemental substance

An elemental substance contains only one chemical element. It may contain individual atoms or molecules: H₂, O₂ and N₂ are elemental substances even though their molecules contain two atoms. Graphite is also an elemental substance.

A compound contains several chemical elements: H₂O and CH₄ are compounds.

Standard enthalpy of formation

Enthalpy change for the synthesis of 1 mole of a compound in its standard state from the appropriate elemental substances in their standard reference states.

The coefficient of the compound formed is 1. Fractional coefficients are possible for the elemental substances. Their standard formation enthalpy is zero when they are in their standard reference states.

Standard reference state of an element

The elemental substance in its most stable state at pressure p° and the temperature T considered.

The standard formation enthalpy of an element in its reference state is zero by convention.

Examples of reference states: H₂(g), Cl₂(g), O₂(g), N₂(g) and graphite. An element's reference state may depend on temperature.

Standard enthalpy of combustion

Enthalpy change associated with complete combustion of one mole of a substance in oxygen, at constant pressure, in the specified standard states. Complete combustion is exothermic and goes to completion.

water produced by combustion is taken as liquid, H₂O(l), unless otherwise stated. Always specify its phase and use matching data. This convention for combustion equations does not mean that water is liquid at every temperature.

Writing a complete combustion equation

Reacting system→Heat to the surroundings

In the cases studied, complete combustion goes to completion and is exothermic: ΔcombH° < 0. Changing the water phase changes the combustion enthalpy.

Bond dissociation enthalpy

Enthalpy change for A–B(g) → A(g) + B(g). Dissociating the bond requires energy: ΔdissH° > 0.

The species are considered in the gas phase, with intermolecular interactions neglected: this isolates the energy associated with the broken bond without adding the effects of condensed-phase intermolecular interactions.

Breaking or forming a bond

Bonds are broken in gaseous species. The reverse operation, forming the bond, changes the sign of the enthalpy.

Enthalpy of dissolution

It describes a substance becoming dissolved, for example A(s) → A(aq). It refers to one mole of dissolved substance under the specified conditions.

Do not confuse dissolution in a solvent with bond dissociation in the gas phase. In a cycle, the states (s), (l), (g) and (aq) are part of the equation.

For an ionic solid, distinguish lattice dissociation, which separates the ions, from solvation, which surrounds them with solvent molecules. In water, this is called hydration. Separation requires energy; solvation releases it. The balance determines the sign of the dissolution enthalpy.

Molecular dissolution, for example glucose dissolving, does not necessarily produce ions: the molecules separate and are then solvated.

Dissociation and solvation of an ionic solid

Example: dissolving NaCl in water. Explore the steps to visualise the two contributions.

NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

Splitting the process into separation then solvation helps understanding. During actual dissolution, the phenomena are coupled. The final drawing zooms in on one ion of each type; the ions remain ions.

EXERCISE 1

Forming benzene from acetylene

Calculate the standard enthalpy of the reaction: 3 C₂H₂(g) → C₆H₆(g).

Data: ΔfH°(C₂H₂,g) = 226.7 kJ·mol⁻¹ ; ΔfH°(C₆H₆,g) = 82.9 kJ·mol⁻¹.

Data: references and notes (formation)

Numerical result: a difference of ±2% is accepted for rounding. Follow the stated sign convention and unit.

Hint 1

Write ΔrH° = Σproducts ν ΔfH° − Σreactants ν ΔfH°.

Detailed solution

For 3 C₂H₂(g) → C₆H₆(g), apply ΔrH° = sum of product formation enthalpies − sum of reactant formation enthalpies, weighted by coefficients.

The reactants contribute 3 × 226.7 = 680.1 kJ·mol⁻¹; the product contributes 82.9 kJ·mol⁻¹. Hence ΔrH° = 82.9−680.1 = −597.2 kJ·mol⁻¹. The factor 3 applies to acetylene; benzene is gaseous in this exercise.

EXERCISE 2

Balancing methane combustion

Write the complete combustion of one mole of gaseous methane in oxygen. The chosen products are CO₂(g) and H₂O(l).

CH₄(g) + a O₂(g) → b CO₂(g) + c H₂O(l)

Hint 1

Balance C, then H, then O.

Solution

CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l).

EXERCISE 3

Calculating a hydrogenation enthalpy

C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g), at 298 K.

En kJ·mol⁻¹ : ΔfH°(C₂H₄,g) = 52.28 ; ΔfH°(C₂H₆,g) = −84.67 ; ΔfH°(H₂,g) = 0.

Data: references and notes (formation)

Numerical result: a difference of ±2% is accepted for rounding. Follow the stated sign convention and unit.

Hint 1

Write the product sum, then subtract the reactant sum.

Detailed solution

For C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g), subtract the reactant standard formation enthalpies from those of the products.

H₂(g) is the elemental substance in its standard reference state: ΔfH° = 0. The reactant sum is therefore 52.28 + 0 = 52.28 kJ·mol⁻¹; the product value is −84.67 kJ·mol⁻¹.

ΔrH° = −84.67 − 52.28 = −136.95 kJ·mol⁻¹ : the reaction is exothermic.
