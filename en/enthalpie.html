The enthalpy function

Many physical or chemical processes occur at atmospheric pressure, treated as constant, particularly when the medium is in contact with open air. To study their energy balance, it is useful to define a suitable thermodynamic potential: enthalpy.

We consider a closed system: an open-air process can be modelled this way if matter exchange with the surroundings is negligible. The imposed external pressure does not prevent volume changes by expansion or compression.

Enthalpy

Thermodynamic potential

Enthalpy is the state function defined by:

H=U+pV

It combines internal energy U with the term pV, where p is the system pressure. This term accounts for pressure–volume work in a constant-pressure balance; pV is not itself the work exchanged.

Why does the enthalpy change give the heat at constant pressure?

The system is closed, only pressure–volume work is exchanged, and changes in macroscopic kinetic and potential energies are negligible. External pressure is constant and the initial and final states are in mechanical equilibrium with the surroundings:

pi=pf=pext

The work received, positive for compression and negative for expansion, is:

W=−pext(Vf−Vi)

The first law then gives:

ΔU=Qp−pextΔV

By definition of H, since the initial and final pressures equal the external pressure:

ΔH=ΔU+Δ(pV)=ΔU+pextΔV

Substituting ΔU, the two pressure–volume work terms cancel:

ΔH=Qp−pextΔV+pextΔV=Qp

Interpreting the sign of ΔH

Under these conditions, the enthalpy change equals the heat exchanged at constant pressure. An exothermic process releases heat: ΔH < 0. An endothermic process receives heat: ΔH > 0.

This relationship applies at atmospheric pressure or any other constant external pressure if the same conditions hold. H is a state function; its change ΔH corresponds to the heat exchanged in this balance.

What does ΔH mean?

H is enthalpy, the state function defined by H = U + pV. Δ denotes its change between two states : ΔH = Hf − Hᵢ. Under the conditions stated above, this change is the heat transferred.

The notations ΔrH°T, ΔfH°T or ΔvapH°T then specify the process type, standard states and temperature. Each symbol is explained in the “Reaction enthalpy” module.

Heat capacity

Heat capacity C

Heat required to raise a constituent's temperature by 1 °C (or 1 K). It is expressed in J·K⁻¹.

δQ = C dT ; Q = C ΔT

These relationships describe heating without reaction or phase transition under specified conditions. Q = C ΔT assumes constant C over the interval. At constant pressure, use Cp.

Capacity Unit Calculation
C J·K⁻¹ Q = C ΔT
Molar Cp,m J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ Q = n Cp,m ΔT
Specific cp J·kg⁻¹·K⁻¹ or J·g⁻¹·K⁻¹ Q = m cp ΔT

For several constituents, add their contributions. The mass or amount of substance must match the unit of the given capacity.

At constant pressure, for a fixed amount of a constituent undergoing no reaction or phase transition:

dH = Cp dT ; Cp = (∂H/∂T)p

We may write Cp(T) = a + bT + cT². Heat capacity is often treated as constant over the interval:

ΔH = Qp = ∑ᵢ nᵢ Cp,m,i ΔT

If temperature dependence is included, integrate Cp(T). Useful antiderivatives are listed in the practical reference.

Heating and phase transitions

Melting and vaporisation require energy to overcome the cohesive forces of condensed phases. Temperature remains constant during a pure substance's phase transition at constant pressure.

Qmelting = n ΔfusH°
Qvaporisation = n ΔvapH°

Understanding latent heat

During melting or vaporisation of a pure substance at constant pressure, the system receives energy while its temperature stays constant: this energy enables the phase transition. The plateau below illustrates this situation. The term latent heat denotes this phase-transition heat; “latent” indicates that the supplied heat does not cause a temperature increase here.

Distinguish the total heat exchanged from the quantity specified per unit amount:

QuantityUnitMeaning
QpJ or kJHeat exchanged by the entire amount undergoing the phase transition.
ΔfusH or ΔvapHJ·mol⁻¹ or kJ·mol⁻¹Phase-transition enthalpy for one mole.
Lfus or LvapJ·kg⁻¹ or J·g⁻¹Specific latent heat, per kilogram or gram.
Qp = n Δphase transitionH = m L
L = Δphase transitionH / M

M is molar mass: with molar ΔH in J·mol⁻¹ and M in kg·mol⁻¹, L is in J·kg⁻¹. In these expressions, n or m is the amount actually transformed, at the phase-transition temperature.

For melting and vaporisation, L is positive and the system receives heat. At the same temperature, the reverse processes release this heat: Qfreezing = −m Lfus and Qcondensation = −m Lvap.

Example: Vaporising the 3 mol of water already at 100 °C requires 3 × 40.7 = 122.1 kJ. The preceding heating from 20 to 100 °C is calculated separately. Use m c ΔT for heating within one phase and m L for the phase transition: ΔT = 0 on the plateau does not mean Q = 0.

Splitting heating into several steps

When the initial and final states span several phases, split the path at each phase transition. At the pressure considered, with melting at 0 °C and boiling at 100 °C, going from ice at Tᵢ < 0 °C to vapour at Tf > 100 °C requires five contributions:

  1. Heat the ice: Q₁ = m cice(0 − Tᵢ).
  2. Melt the ice: Q₂ = n ΔfusH.
  3. Heat liquid water: Q₃ = m cwater(100 − 0).
  4. Vaporise water: Q₄ = n ΔvapH.
  5. Heat the vapour: Q₅ = m cvapour(Tf − 100).
Qtotal = Q₁ + Q₂ + Q₃ + Q₄ + Q₅

Use the heat capacity of the phase present and the phase-transition enthalpy at the step's temperature. Reversing a phase transition changes the enthalpy sign: condensation releases the heat absorbed by vaporisation at the same temperature.

EXERCISE 1

Vaporising three moles of water

Calculate the heat required to vaporise 3 moles of water initially at 20 °C.

Data: Cp,m(H₂O,l) = 75.3 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ ; ΔvapH° = 40.7 kJ·mol⁻¹.

Data: references and notes (water)

Numerical result: a difference of ±2% is accepted for rounding. Follow the stated sign convention and unit.

Hint 1

There are two steps: heat the liquid, then change phase at constant temperature.

Hint 2

The term Cp,m ΔT is in J·mol⁻¹ here; vaporisation enthalpy is in kJ·mol⁻¹.

Detailed solution

1. Separate the processes. Liquid water is first heated from 20 to 100 °C, then vaporised at 100 °C. Temperature stays constant during vaporisation.

2. Heating. Q₁ = nCp,mΔT = 3 × 75.3 × (100−20) = 18 072 J = 18.072 kJ.

3. Vaporisation. Q₂ = nΔvapH = 3 × 40.7 = 122.1 kJ.

4. Total heat. Q = Q₁ + Q₂ = 140.172 kJ, approximately 140.2 kJ. The positive sign indicates heat received by the water.

EXERCISE 2

Isolating the heating step

Heat 3 mol of liquid water from 20 to 100 °C at constant pressure, without vaporisation. Cp,m = 75.3 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ is constant.

Data: references and notes (water)

Calculate the heat received by the 3 mol of water, in kJ.

Numerical result: a difference of ±2% is accepted for rounding. Follow the stated sign convention and unit.

Hint 1

Distinguish molar heat capacity from the capacity of the entire water sample.

Detailed solution

Molar heat capacity refers to one mole; for 3 mol, C = nCp,m = 3 × 75.3 = 225.9 J·K⁻¹. The temperature change is 100−20 = 80 K.

At constant pressure, assuming constant heat capacity, Q = nCp,m(Tf−Ti) = 225.9 × 80 = 18 072 J = 18.072 kJ. The water warms up: Q is positive.

EXERCISE 3

Reversing a phase transition

At the same temperature of 100 °C, condense 3 mol of water vapour into liquid. Use ΔvapH = +40.7 kJ·mol⁻¹, as in the exercise.

Data: references and notes (water)

Calculate the signed heat exchanged by the 3 mol of water, in kJ.

Numerical result: a difference of ±2% is accepted for rounding. Follow the stated sign convention and unit.

Hint 1

Reversing the phase transition reverses the sign.

Detailed solution

Condensation is the reverse of vaporisation at the same temperature: ΔcondH = −ΔvapH = −40.7 kJ·mol⁻¹.

For 3 mol, Q = nΔcondH = 3 × (−40.7) = −122.1 kJ. Water releases heat; do not add a heating term because the process occurs at 100 °C.
