Further study — From energy to equilibrium

1. Why is the first law not enough?

The first law requires conservation of energy. By itself, it does not say whether a process will occur spontaneously in a given direction. At atmospheric pressure, an ice cube in surroundings maintained above its melting temperature can melt spontaneously while absorbing heat: melting is endothermic.

“Spontaneous” here means thermodynamically favoured under the imposed conditions. It says nothing about the rate: a favoured process may be very slow.

2. Entropy and the second law

Entropy is a state function, expressed in J·K⁻¹. It links the macroscopic description to possible energy distributions and microscopic configurations compatible with the system's state. Reducing it to the word “disorder” does not allow reliable prediction of change.

For a reversible process, its elementary change is:

dS=δQrevT

This relationship uses heat along a reversible path; that term cannot be replaced by the actual heat of an arbitrary irreversible path.

Second law

The entropy of an isolated system does not decrease: ΔS ≥ 0. It increases during irreversible change and stays constant in the reversible limit. At stable equilibrium, it is maximal among accessible states under the imposed constraints.

If a system exchanges energy or matter with its surroundings, its entropy may decrease. The surroundings must then also be considered: it is the entropy of the isolated whole that does not decrease.

3. The third law: a reference for entropy

Third law

For a perfect crystal with a unique ground state, entropy tends to zero as temperature tends to 0 K. This reference, combined with heat-capacity measurements and phase-transition contributions, allows tabulated entropies to be established.

A standard molar entropy S°m is expressed in J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ for a specified phase and temperature. Unlike standard formation enthalpy, an elemental substance's standard molar entropy is generally not zero at ordinary temperatures.

The third law provides a reference; it is not needed to define F and G.

4. Why several thermodynamic potentials?

We have met U and H. Two other state functions combine energy and entropy:

= + pV
= U − TS
= H − TS

Thermodynamic potential

F is Helmholtz energy, also denoted A. G is Gibbs energy. These four potentials are not four independent energy stores: they are connected by their definitions.

For a closed system with no work other than pressure–volume work, in contact with reservoirs imposing the stated constraints, the usual criteria are:

Constraints and stable equilibrium
ConstraintsRelevant potentialSpontaneous change
Constant temperature and volumeFF decreases to a minimum at stable equilibrium
Constant temperature and pressureGG decreases to a minimum at stable equilibrium

These minima are sought among accessible states, respecting conservation of matter and allowed reactions. Not all potentials always decrease.

Further detail: criteria involving U and H

For a simple compressible system under the same material constraints, U is minimal at stable equilibrium at fixed entropy and volume; H is minimal at fixed entropy and pressure. These entropy constraints differ from those imposed by a thermostat.

For an isolated system at fixed U and V, S is instead maximised. U remains constant: it does not decrease during this change.

IUPAC · Alberty (2001), thermodynamic-potential framework (advanced text).

5. The link with the equilibrium constant

For a specified reaction at the same temperature T:

ΔrG° = ΔrH° − TΔrS° = −RT ln K

R is the ideal gas constant, T the absolute temperature in kelvins, and K the thermodynamic equilibrium constant, dimensionless, defined using activities. If R is in J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹, molar energies must be in J·mol⁻¹ and molar reaction entropy in J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹.

Standard reaction entropy is calculated from standard molar entropies: the product sum minus the reactant sum, weighted by stoichiometric coefficients.

Key point

Enthalpy contributes to equilibrium, but it is not enough to determine K : the entropy term also contributes.

Second level: ΔrG, ΔrG° and the reaction quotient

ΔrG° refers to standard states. ΔrG concerns the mixture in its current state and depends on composition through the reaction quotient Qr. Do not confuse Qr with heat Q.

ΔrG = ΔrG° + RT ln Qr = RT ln QrK
  • If Qr < K, ΔrG < 0: the forward direction is favoured.
  • If Qr > K, ΔrG > 0: the reverse direction is favoured.
  • At chemical equilibrium, Qr = K and ΔrG = 0.

At constant temperature and pressure, dG = ΔrG dξ for a single reaction. ΔrG = 0 at equilibrium does not mean G = 0 or ΔrG° = 0. The latter equality corresponds to the special case K = 1.

The relationship between K and ΔrG° remains valid for equilibrium reached in a rigid vessel: do not replace ΔrG° with ΔrF° in this relationship.
